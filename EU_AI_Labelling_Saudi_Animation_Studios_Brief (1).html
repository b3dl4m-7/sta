What decides your labelling burden: is the work "evidently" fiction?

Three regimes now sit on top of a Saudi studio at once: EU law if the work reaches European audiences, commissioner rules written by Netflix for every production partner and vendor, and Saudi frameworks — SDAIA guidance hardening through the Year of AI, PDPL, and CST cloud rules underneath it all. None of the three asks whether you used AI. All three ask whether you can show how.

Reel One · Does Brussels Reach You?

01 · The Reach TestAnswer three questions.

The Act applies to providers and deployers when the AI output is used in the Union — including content accessible to EU audiences on the open web. Domicile in Riyadh is not a shield. Walk through it:

Interactive · 60 seconds

Is your studio in scope of Article 50?

Question 1 of 3

Will the finished work be distributed, streamed, or accessible to audiences in the EU — including via a global platform?

Question 2 of 3

Does any AI system in your pipeline generate or manipulate image, audio, or video — including upscaling, cleanup, style transfer, or synthetic voice?

Question 3 of 3

Is the work evidently fictional or artistic — animation, drama, satire — rather than informational, commercial, or documentary-style content?

In scope — reduced regime

Article 50(4) gives evidently artistic, creative, satirical and fictional works a lighter path: you must still disclose the existence of AI-generated or manipulated content, but in a way that doesn't hamper enjoyment — accompanying materials or credits, not a watermark across every frame.

Do not relax. The final EU guidance interprets these categories narrowly, and content that is informative or commercial — your trailers, promos, behind-the-scenes marketing — cannot use the reduced regime. Your film may qualify; your ad campaign for it may not.

In scope — full obligations

Final outputs must be marked and machine-detectable as AI-generated. Deepfake-class content — anything resembling real people, places, or events that could pass as authentic — needs clear disclosure. Marketing and commercial content gets no artistic discount.

Good news from the final guidelines: closed-loop production workflows are recognised — intermediate stages don't need marking. Only what leaves the building must carry it.

Likely out of EU scope — today

No EU audience, no EU obligation. But two cautions: global platforms make "regional only" fragile — one licensing deal changes your answer — and Saudi frameworks (SDAIA deepfake and generative AI guidance, hardening through 2026) apply regardless.

Out of Article 50 — verify that claim

"Fully manual" is a strong statement in 2026. Toon Boom's Ember, Adobe's Firefly features, plugin AI in compositing — embedded AI counts. Before certifying a no-AI pipeline to anyone, audit what's actually switched on. Reel Three below shows why.

Reel Two · The Pipeline Carve-Out

02 · Where Marking BitesInside the pipeline: nothing.
At delivery: everything.

The single most studio-friendly line in the final EU guidelines: outputs used in closed-loop production workflows for film, animation and games don't need marking at intermediate stages. Only the final output must be marked and detectable. The B2B carve-out holds if outputs stay inside the organisation, protected by safeguards — cloud isolation, role-based access.

Read the fork carefully. The same AI-assisted footage exits your pipeline twice: as the film (credits-level disclosure under the artistic regime) and as the trailer (full labelling — commercial content is excluded from the carve-out, narrowly interpreted). One pipeline, two obligations.

Clearly fantastical content — dragons, humans flying unaided — falls outside the deepfake definition. Your talking camel is safe. Your photoreal Riyadh street scene is not. EU draft guidelines, deepfake scope — applied to animation

02b · Your Gates Are the CheckpointsYou already run five approvals.
Ride them.

A standard animation production runs Development → Pre-Production → Shot-Production → Post-Production, punctuated by approval gates: script, characters & locations, animatic with colour, 3D animation, final pilot — roughly 50 + 30 + 50 days of gated work. The mistake would be building AI governance as a sixth parallel process. The move is to attach one AI question to each gate you already hold:

Five gates, five questions, zero new meetings. Each approval you already run gains one AI line-item, signed by the AI Owner. Gate 5 is where the Reel Two fork gets decided — and note the branch your own process chart makes explicit: marketing & distribution splits off the animatic early, which means the promo pipeline needs its own labelling track from that point, not as an afterthought at delivery.

Reel Four · The AI Owner

04 · One Name on the SlateWhat the "AI Owner"
actually does all day.

Both the EU compliance model and Netflix's escalation logic assume someone answers for AI use — not a committee. In a studio, the role is closer to a line producer for provenance than an IT function:

Keeps the inventory

Every tool with AI capability, per version, per seat — including what's dormant in preferences. Updated at each software upgrade, because upgrades are where AI arrives silently.

Owns the shot log

Scene-level record: which shots touched AI, which tool, what kind of processing, who approved the result. The exact granularity Netflix's framework describes.

Gates the escalations

Decides what's low-risk ideation versus what needs commissioner sign-off — final-frame generation, any talent likeness, any personal data, any third-party IP.

Signs the warranties

When the contract asks the studio to certify its AI use, this person's records are what makes the signature honest — and what the E&O insurer will ask to see.

For a 20-person studio this is a hat, not a hire — but a named hat, with the register as a standing deliverable. Their signature appears exactly five times per production: once at each gate in the 02b chart. The moment it's "everyone's job," it's no one's evidence.

Reel Five · The Netflix Clause Watchlist

05 · Read Before SigningSix clauses that should
raise eyebrows in Riyadh.

Netflix's Saudi footprint is deep — an exclusive multi-year partnership with Myrkott (the Masameer studio, Netflix's largest Arab-animation investment at signing, with first-look rights on upcoming projects) and an eight-film slate with Telfaz11. Netflix's published GenAI framework already binds production partners and their vendors: disclose intended AI use to your Netflix contact, written approval for anything touching final deliverables, talent likeness, personal data or third-party IP, and custom AI workflows must pass the guidelines at every pipeline step. International commissioning for the region typically runs through EU-domiciled entities — which means an EU counterparty, EU-anchored obligations, and contract language built for the Act. When the next long-form agreement lands, these are the clauses to slow down on:

The blanket AI-compliance warrantyEyebrow 1

"Producer warrants all deliverables comply with all applicable AI transparency laws, including Regulation (EU) 2024/1689." One sentence transfers the entire EU compliance burden — including rules still being clarified through guidelines and codes of practice — onto the studio.

Push for: a mutual-cooperation clause instead — studio warrants accuracy of its AI-usage disclosures; platform owns end-user labelling on its service. You can honestly warrant your records. You cannot honestly warrant Brussels.
Uncapped AI indemnitiesEyebrow 2

Indemnify the platform for "any claim arising from the use of artificial intelligence in the production" — unlimited, surviving delivery, covering training-data claims against tools the studio doesn't control (a model vendor's dataset dispute becomes your liability).

Push for: caps tied to the production fee; carve-outs for claims arising from the tool vendor's training data; indemnity limited to breaches of your own disclosed AI-use schedule.
Chain-of-title on AI-assisted framesEyebrow 3

Standard chain-of-title asks you to warrant you own everything. Purely AI-generated elements have contested copyright status in most jurisdictions — you may be warranting ownership of frames no law clearly lets you own. Netflix's own principles require that outputs don't replicate identifiable copyrighted material; the contract version of that idea can be far broader.

Push for: a schedule distinguishing human-authored, AI-assisted (human-directed and modified), and AI-generated assets — with warranty language calibrated per category, not one blanket claim.
Audit rights into your pipelineEyebrow 4

The platform can audit your AI usage records "on reasonable notice." Fair in principle — Netflix's framework already expects scene-level logs. The eyebrow: scope. Unbounded audit language reaches your other clients' confidential projects sitting in the same tracker and the same cloud.

Push for: audit scope limited to the commissioned production's records; your shot-level AI log defined as the audit artefact — which is another reason to build it properly.
Synthetic voice & performance consent flow-downEyebrow 5

Netflix requires consent for generating or materially altering performances — even minor changes like visual ADR. For animation, dubbing and Arabic-localisation workflows using voice AI (ElevenLabs sits inside Ember's stack), the studio must hold documented consent from every voice artist whose sound feeds any synthetic process — and flow that duty to every dubbing vendor.

Push for: a consent-register obligation you can actually operate, and template consent language in your standard talent agreements now, before the project that needs it retroactively.
Tool restrictions you can't see intoEyebrow 6

"Only enterprise-secured AI tools that do not store, reuse, or train on production data" — Netflix's stated principle, reasonable on its face. But the studio is being asked to warrant the internal data practices of third-party software vendors. Toon Boom publishes a no-training statement for Ember; many tools don't.

Push for: compliance defined as using tools from an agreed schedule (updated by notice), with vendor attestations accepted as sufficient evidence — so you warrant diligence, not other companies' server behaviour.
Reel Six · The Relationship Cascade

06 · EU ↔ KSADisclosure flows downhill.
So does liability.

The middle position is the exposed one. The studio absorbs obligations from above and must push them below — to dubbing houses, outsourced comp, freelancers on personal Midjourney accounts. A freelancer's undisclosed AI use becomes the studio's breached warranty. Vendor onboarding needs an AI-use declaration the way it already needs an NDA.

Reel Seven · Build It Secure From Day One

07 · The Micro-Studio StackSmall studio. Sovereign data.
Provable pipeline.

For a startup studio in Riyadh, the security question and the disclosure question are the same question: a well-architected asset database is your audit trail. And yes — generative AI use should itself be bound by the disclosure discipline even when it's deployed to protect the database: an AI tool watching your infrastructure is still an AI system on your inventory. The Saudi layer adds real constraints: PDPL on personal data and cross-border transfers, CST's cloud framework classifying data and providers, NCA controls beneath — with in-Kingdom regions (AWS Riyadh, Oracle Jeddah) satisfying residency where it's required. Build in this order:

In-Kingdom cloud region, classified from day one

Host production data in a local region; classify what you hold (personal data of cast/crew vs. project assets vs. anything government-linked — the last changes everything under CST/NCA rules). Startups lose audits on documentation, not technology.

Isolation + role-based access — the carve-out conditions

Cloud isolation and RBAC aren't just security hygiene; they're literally the EU's B2B carve-out conditions for unmarked intermediate content. Your security architecture is your regulatory posture.

The AI inventory register

Every tool, version, AI capability, default state, vendor's data/training terms. One page at first. Reviewed at every software update — because updates are where AI features arrive unannounced.

Shot-level provenance in the tracker

Custom fields in Producer / Flow / ftrack: AI-used (y/n), tool, process type, approver. Thirty minutes of configuration that becomes your Netflix log, your EU evidence, and your E&O submission.

PDPL mapping for people-data in AI flows

Voice recordings feeding dubbing tools, cast images in reference libraries, crew data in schedulers — each is personal data in an AI process. Map it, minimise it, document transfer grounds before any tool sends it abroad.

Consent templates before you need them

Voice and likeness consent language in standard talent agreements from the first contract — retrofitting consent across a delivered series is the expensive version.

Reel Eight · The Questions Nobody Is Asking Yet

08 · Open QuestionsEight things to raise
before your next production.

Q1 · Insurance

Will E&O insurers exclude AI-generated assets without provenance records — and will premiums start pricing the quality of your AI log?

Q2 · Festivals & Broadcasters

Festival entry forms and broadcaster delivery specs are adding AI declarations. Who at the studio answers them, and against which record?

Q3 · Arabic Synthetic Voice

Voice AI makes Arabic dubbing dramatically cheaper. Whose voice trained the model, who consented, and who owns the resulting performance?

Q4 · The KSA Copyright Paradox

Saudi's new copyright law eases the use of works for AI development while the EU tightens output disclosure. Can a studio train on its own back-catalogue and still warrant clean chain-of-title abroad?

Q5 · Silent Updates

When a tool update enables AI by default mid-production (the Harmony 24/25 lesson), does your change-management even notice — and who re-certifies the pipeline?

Q6 · C2PA in Delivery Specs

Content Credentials (cryptographic provenance) are moving from press photography toward streaming delivery requirements. Should your renders carry them before you're asked?

Q7 · Who Audits the AI Owner?

A register nobody verifies is a liability with a nice cover page. Internal audit — or the finance function — needs the AI log in its annual scope.

Q8 · Government-Linked Work

Commissions from giga-projects and public entities pull NCA critical-systems controls and stricter residency into your pipeline. Does your cloud architecture survive that client?
