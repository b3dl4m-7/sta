Disclosure only works if it is specific. "This post used AI" tells a reader nothing about what was machine-made, what was checked, and who stands behind it. What follows is the full statement, then the variants and code snippets to reuse across formats.

The Statement
◆ AI Transparency Disclosure Art. 50 EU AI Act · SDAIA Generative AI Guidelines

This content was produced with AI assistance under human editorial oversight.

The text was drafted using a large language model. Research direction, source selection, verification of facts, figures and regulatory status, analytical framing, correction and final approval were carried out by the named author, who takes full editorial responsibility for what is published.

This is AI-assisted authorship, not autonomous generation — a distinction the transparency rules themselves turn on, since the labelling obligation for informational text applies most stringently where content reaches the public without human editorial review.

Where this document is published in HTML, machine-readable provenance markers are embedded in its metadata, so the disclosure survives copying, scraping and re-syndication rather than existing only as visible text a reader may never reach.

Part TwoMachine-Readable Markers

This is the part most people skip, and it is the part that matters. A visible label is stripped the moment content is copied. Metadata travels with the file — which is precisely what "embedded markers" means in the regulatory language.

Paste this block into the <head> of any page you publish.

HTML · Metadata Block
<!-- AI TRANSPARENCY DISCLOSURE — machine-readable markers -->
<meta name="ai-generated" content="true">
<meta name="ai-disclosure" content="AI-assisted. Drafted with an AI language model; researched, verified, edited and approved under named human editorial oversight.">
<meta name="ai-generator" content="Large language model">
<meta name="ai-human-oversight" content="true">
<meta name="ai-oversight-party" content="Syed Tanveer Ali FCCA, CMA">
<meta name="ai-content-scope" content="Text. No AI-generated images, audio or video. No synthetic media.">
<meta name="ai-disclosure-date" content="2026-08-09">
<meta name="generator" content="AI-assisted authoring with human editorial review">
On file formats: for images, provenance lives in EXIF, IPTC or XMP fields rather than HTML meta tags, and C2PA Content Credentials are the emerging cross-industry standard for cryptographically signed provenance. If you begin publishing AI-generated visuals rather than text, that is the direction to move in — a visible corner stamp alone will not survive a screenshot.

Part FourWhere the Line Actually Falls

Worth being clear on this, because over-labelling is its own failure. The transparency obligation is heaviest in three situations:

Synthetic media of people or events — generated or manipulated image, audio or video that could pass as real. This is the core case, and it is where "no exceptions" genuinely applies.

Systems that talk to people — a chatbot must say it is a machine. There is no editorial-review defence here; the disclosure is structural.

Informational text published without human review — where AI-written text about matters of public interest goes out with nobody checking it. Note the conditional. Text that is drafted by a model but genuinely reviewed, corrected and owned by a named person sits differently — which is why the wording above says assisted and names the reviewer rather than hiding behind a generic tag.

If everything you publish carries an identical label regardless of how it was made, the label stops carrying information. That is the criticism industry has levelled at the European approach, and it is a fair one. Precision in the disclosure is what keeps it meaningful.

Not legal advice. This is a practical drafting aid reflecting a reasonable reading of published transparency expectations as at August 2026.
